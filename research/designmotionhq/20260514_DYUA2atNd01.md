# Peak-end rule: polish the peak and the ending

**Caption:** not captured yet
**Hook:** Two flows with the same experience end up with very different memories.

## Main rule
People judge a flow mostly by its most intense moment and its last moment, not by the average of all steps. Plan one intentional positive peak in each key flow and make the final screen a clear, warm success; above all, make sure the flow never ends on an error or dead end without a recovery path.

## Before / after
1. **Same average, different ending (0:00-0:02).** Path A and Path B have the same shape and the same average (65); A drops sharply at the end, B rises. Memory for A shows 0/100 at the moment it ends (animated). Why: memory weighs the final moment heavily, so a bad last step overrides an otherwise average experience.
2. **Cold vs joyful checkout ending (0:11-0:20).** Same checkout (subtotal $48.00, shipping $4.99, total $52.99). Cold ending: the form fades out and a small muted check with "Order placed." appears. Joyful ending: a large success check pops in with a confetti burst, headline "You're all set!", a delivery chip ("Tomorrow · free") and "Confirmation sent to your inbox". Memory bars: 58 vs 94. Why: the end screen is the moment that gets stored; a confident success state plus concrete reassurance (delivery date, confirmation sent) closes the loop and creates a positive peak.
3. **Intentional peak (0:21-0:29).** A flow list of neutral steps (Browse "Looking for size 9", Filter "Narrows to 4 results", Add to cart "Standard interaction", Checkout "Pay") followed by a highlighted "Surprise upgrade + Free express shipping" row tagged PEAK. Caption "Plant a peak. One is enough." Why: one unexpected positive moment stands out against routine steps and becomes the remembered high point (examples in voice: filters "clicking" into the right results, a shipping date that beats expectation).
4. **Bad ending kills the flow (0:30-0:37).** Steps Sign up, Verify email, Pick a plan, Add payment check off and a "Memory rating" bar rises to 85/100; then "Payment failed. Card declined · try another method" appears in the danger colour and the rating drops to 22/100; "The 99% that worked? Gone." Why: the error is both the most intense and the last moment, so it defines the memory of the whole onboarding.

## Do
- Identify the end state of every key flow (purchase, sign-up, upload, submission) and design a full success screen: clear confirmation, what happens next, concrete details.
- Plant one deliberate positive peak (a surprise perk, a result that beats expectation, a satisfying "aha" moment) rather than polishing every step equally.
- Audit the final interaction of each flow for failure states; make errors at the end recoverable on the spot.

## Don't
- Don't end a successful flow with a tiny, cold confirmation line.
- Don't spread effort evenly while neglecting the finish.
- Don't let a flow end on friction, an unexplained error or a dead end.

## Voice-over summary
- The brain does not average an experience; it keeps the peak moment and the end. This is the peak-end rule, the same for a two-second flow or a two-day trip.
- Two identical 60-second checkouts: one ends with a celebration animation, one just stops; the first is claimed to rate 40% higher in memory.
- Design intentional peaks: the moment filters produce the right results, a shipping date better than expected. One delight beats five neutral interactions.
- It cuts both ways: a perfect onboarding ending in a payment error is remembered only as the error; "The 99% that worked is gone."

## Notes for the skill
- Evidence: the peak-end rule comes from Kahneman, Fredrickson and colleagues (1993 cold-water study; Redelmeier and Kahneman colonoscopy studies). The effect is well supported; the specific "+40%" and the 58/94 scores in the video are illustrative and internally inconsistent (+36 points is +62% relative). Do not cite them as data.
- A good ending is mainly substance, not decoration: confirm the outcome in a heading, show the reference (order number), what happens next (delivery date, e-mail sent) and a next action. Celebration motion (confetti, check pop) is optional seasoning.
- Motion: keep celebration short (under about 1 s), do not block the next action, and replace confetti/particle bursts with a static success state under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`. Move focus to the success heading and announce it (`role="status"` or focus on an `h1`).
- Reserve celebration for meaningful completions (first purchase, finishing onboarding); celebrating routine actions every time loses its peak value and can annoy.
- End-of-flow errors: if the last step can fail (payment), keep entered data, explain the cause in plain language and offer the fix in place ("try another method", as the demo's error text does). A recoverable error followed by success restores a positive ending.
- A "surprise upgrade" must be genuine; fake scarcity or manipulative "gifts" are dark patterns.
