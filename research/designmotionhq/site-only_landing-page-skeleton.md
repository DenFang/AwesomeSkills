# Landing page skeleton: five sections in a fixed order

**Caption:** none (this video is published on the pattern page only; no matching reel on the Instagram profile as of 2026-10-24)
**Hook:** "Your landing page is broken", followed by a five-section fix.

## Main rule
Structure a landing page as Hero → Social proof → Problem → Solution → Final CTA. The hero answers what / who / why within about 3 seconds with one headline, one subhead and one CTA; proof sits directly under it; the pain is named before the solution; the solution lists at most three outcome-framed benefits; the final CTA repeats the hero button exactly.

## Before / after
- **Hero.** Bad: carousel/slider or several competing messages ("No hero carousels, nobody scrolls them"). Good: one headline (what), one subhead naming the audience and a credibility hint (who, why), one primary CTA ("Start free →"), plus a product visual. On mobile the four stack vertically (text, CTA, visual); on desktop text and CTA sit left, visual right, matching left-to-right scanning. Why: a single message is fully read in the few seconds before the visitor decides to stay; rotating slides hide all but one message.
- **Proof placement.** Bad ("Not this"): proof at the very bottom of the page. Good ("Do this"): proof block directly under the hero: logo row "Trusted by teams at", one star-rated testimonial with name and role, one hard number counter ("Teams shipping with us 2,847"). Why: trust is decided early; proof must be in view before the visitor starts doubting.
- **Problem before solution.** Bad: jumping straight to the product. Good: a section quantifying the pain in time, money and frustration (3 weeks, $8,400, 12 revision rounds). Why: "No pain felt → no care given"; the solution only matters relative to a felt cost.
- **Features vs outcomes.** Bad: feature labels ("Advanced automation", "AI-driven personalization", "Enterprise-grade tooling"). Good: each rewritten as a measurable result ("Save 10 hours a week", "2x your conversion rate", "Ship in a single day"), capped at three. Why: outcomes describe the visitor's gain directly; three items stay memorable.
- **Final CTA.** Good: the bottom section ("Ready to ship?") reuses the hero button with identical label and styling ("Start free →"). Why: visitors who were not ready at the top meet a familiar, already-understood action at the bottom ("Repetition isn't lazy. It's conversion.").

## Do
- Keep the five sections in order: Hero, Proof, Problem, Solution, Final CTA.
- Hero: one headline, one subhead, one primary button, one visual; answer what, who and why.
- Stack hero content vertically on mobile; place text left and visual right on desktop.
- Put logos, one testimonial and one hard number directly under the hero.
- Quantify the problem with concrete costs (time, money, effort) before presenting the solution.
- Write at most three benefits, each as a measurable outcome.
- Repeat the exact same CTA (label, style, destination) at the bottom.

## Don't
- Don't use carousels or sliders in the hero.
- Don't bury social proof at the bottom.
- Don't pitch the solution before establishing the pain.
- Don't list features or more than three benefits.
- Don't invent a different final CTA with new wording or styling.

## Voice-over summary
- Promises a fix for a broken landing page in five sections.
- Hero must say what, who and why in three seconds: one headline, one subhead, one button; stack on mobile, left-to-right on desktop; no carousels.
- Social proof goes right after the hero (logos, testimonials or numbers) because trust is decided in under 10 seconds.
- Agitate the pain before selling the cure; without felt pain the solution doesn't matter.
- Three benefits max, outcomes not features ("Save 10 hours a week" beats "advanced automation"); final CTA repeats the hero button in colour and text, because repetition converts.

## Notes for the skill
- Treat this as a conversion-copy structure for marketing landing pages, not a universal rule for product, docs or app home pages. Long-form pages often add sections (how it works, pricing, FAQ, objections) between Solution and Final CTA; the order of the five core beats still holds.
- Hero layout hint: mobile single column (headline → subhead → CTA → visual); desktop two columns ~50/50 or text-heavy left. Keep the CTA above the fold on common mobile heights.
- Primary CTA: use one accent colour reserved for the primary action and keep its label and destination identical at top and bottom; secondary actions should be visually quieter.
- Proof: real logos and attributable testimonials (name, role, company); avoid invented or unverifiable numbers. Animated counters should respect `prefers-reduced-motion` and render the final number for screen readers (do not let assistive tech read intermediate values; put the final value in the DOM).
- Pain metrics: concrete and specific numbers beat adjectives; keep them honest.
- Timing claims ("3 seconds", "under 10 seconds") are heuristics, not measured thresholds. NN/g data shows many visitors leave within 10-20 seconds unless the value proposition is clear, which supports the intent, not the exact numbers.
- Carousels: auto-rotating carousels also raise accessibility issues (WCAG 2.2.2 requires pause/stop for moving content over 5 seconds); avoid them in the hero.
