# Gradient design: four rules from cheap to premium

**Caption:** The perfect gradient ☝️
**Hook:** Two "Get Started" buttons with gradients; one looks cheap and one looks expensive, and the difference is a few construction rules.

## Main rule
Build gradients from neighbouring hues (hue travel of at most about 60° on the colour wheel), give linear gradients a diagonal direction (135° is the video's pick), prefer multi-point (mesh/radial) gradients for surfaces, and apply gradients to headlines only while body text stays a solid colour.

## Before / after
1. **Hook buttons (0:00-0:02).** Cheap: a "Get Started" button whose gradient sweeps across many hues (a full-spectrum ramp through warm and cool ends). Premium: the same button with two neighbouring hues and a soft glow. Why: wide hue travel produces several competing colour bands and visual noise; a narrow hue range reads as one colour family with lighting variation.
2. **Adjacent vs distant hues (0:04-0:14).** Good: stops 60° apart on the wheel, labelled "Harmonious". Bad: stops 180° apart, labelled "Clashing", with a muddy/garish transition zone in the middle. Why: neighbouring hues blend smoothly because intermediate colours stay saturated and plausible; complementary hues interpolated in sRGB pass through a desaturated, greyish or overly bright middle (the page calls it a muddy dead zone).
3. **Direction (0:15-0:28).** Four identical two-stop gradients at 0° ("flat"), 45°, 90° and 135°; the 135° card is kept and labelled "The Premium Angle", the others dim. Why (claimed): a diagonal reads like light falling from the top-left, which adds a sense of depth; vertical and horizontal ramps look like default fills.
4. **Linear vs mesh (0:29-0:37).** Bad: a plain linear 2-colour panel. Good: a dark panel with 3 glowing colour points blended organically ("Mesh, 3 color points"), then applied as the header of a product card ("Acme Creative Studio", Get Started / Learn More). Why: several soft light sources create depth and ambiance that a single ramp cannot; it works as a background mood rather than a flat surface.
5. **Gradient text (0:38-0:47).** Bad ("× UNREADABLE"): a multi-hue gradient on both the headline and the body paragraph. Good ("✓ STATEMENT"): a narrow-hue gradient on the headline only, with body text in solid muted colour; annotations "Gradient headline →" and "← Solid body text". Why: large display text survives the contrast variation along a gradient; small body text loses legibility where contrast dips, and colour shifts across a line slow reading.

## Do
- Keep hue travel between stops at about 60° or less.
- Keep lightness changing in one direction across the gradient (page).
- Prefer a diagonal angle (135° in the video) for linear gradients on surfaces and buttons.
- Use multi-point or radial glows as ambiance behind content.
- Restrict gradient text to large headlines; keep body text solid.
- Add subtle grain (page: 2-3% noise) and test on a low-quality screen to catch banding.

## Don't
- Sweep a gradient across the whole hue wheel or between complementary hues.
- Put body text in a gradient, or on the mid-transition zone of a gradient background.
- Default to 0° or 90° ramps when the gradient is a hero surface.
- Cover content with a full-bleed linear wash (page).

## Voice-over summary
- The same gradient can look cheap or expensive depending on how it is built.
- Rule 1: stay within 60° on the colour wheel; close hues blend, distant hues clash and create noise.
- Rule 2: direction matters; 0° feels flat, 45° and 90° are forgettable, 135° is called "the premium angle" for depth.
- Rule 3: mesh gradients with several colour points give organic transitions and depth a two-colour linear gradient cannot.
- Rule 4: gradient on headlines only; keep body text solid for readability.

## Notes for the skill
- Colour-independent rule: express it as hue distance between stops (≤ 60°), monotonic lightness, and roles (accent family for the gradient, muted solid colour for body text). Derive stops from the project's accent token, e.g. accent and accent shifted by 20-60° in OKLCH.
- Interpolate in a perceptual space to avoid the grey dead zone: `linear-gradient(in oklch 135deg, var(--accent-a), var(--accent-b))` (CSS Color 4; provide an sRGB fallback). Use `longer hue`/`shorter hue` deliberately.
- CSS angle convention: `0deg` points to the top (gradient runs bottom → top), `90deg` runs left → right, `135deg` runs top-left → bottom-right. The video's "0° flat" card is a vertical ramp; describe angles by direction in specs to avoid confusion across tools (Figma uses a different angle origin).
- "135° is the premium angle" is a stylistic heuristic, not a rule; consistency with the product's implied light source matters more than the specific angle.
- Mesh gradients in CSS: layer 2-4 `radial-gradient()`s with large blur-like falloffs over a dark base, or use an SVG/canvas; keep them behind content, low contrast, and static or very slow (respect `prefers-reduced-motion` if animated).
- Gradient text: `background-clip: text; color: transparent;` hides text from forced-colors mode and can fail contrast; check WCAG contrast at the lowest-contrast point of the gradient (4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text ≥ 24px or 18.66px bold), set a solid `color` fallback and handle `@media (forced-colors: active)`.
- Never place body text over the mid-transition zone of a gradient background; add a solid or scrimmed surface behind text instead.
- Banding: add a subtle noise overlay (page suggests 2-3%) and avoid long, low-contrast ramps on large areas.
