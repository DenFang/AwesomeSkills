# Dark patterns: confirmshaming, hidden costs, roach motel

**Caption:** "Did you know?" (no other text besides hashtags).
**Hook:** An "Unsubscribe" button that dodges the cursor so it can never be clicked; that is a dark pattern.

## Main rule
Never design choices that work against the user: the decline option must be worded neutrally and be as visible as the accept option, the full price must be shown up front, and leaving (unsubscribe, cancel) must be as easy as joining.

## Before / after
The reel shows only the bad versions; the good versions below are the direct inverse implied by the narration.
- **Evasive control (hook).** Bad: the "Unsubscribe" button jumps away from the pointer, leaving motion-blurred ghost copies. Good: the opt-out control stays put and is reachable. Why: a control that cannot be targeted removes the user's choice entirely.
- **Confirmshaming.** Bad: popup "Save 50% today!" with a large, high-contrast accept button "Yes, I want to save!" and a tiny muted text link "No thanks, I prefer paying full price" (labelled "Guilt-tripping" on screen). Good: two options with equal weight and neutral wording ("Subscribe" / "No thanks"). Why: shame-laden wording plus low visual weight pushes users to accept out of guilt rather than intent.
- **Hidden costs.** Bad: cart shows "Awesome Product 29€, TOTAL 29€"; shipping 9€, service fee 5€ and taxes 4€ appear only later, and the total jumps to 47€ (+62%). Good: show the all-in price (or a clear estimate of fees) from the first price display. Why: the low anchor hooks the user and the sunk-cost of an almost-finished checkout makes them accept the surprise.
- **Roach motel.** Bad: "Sign up free" is one click, but leaving takes 8 steps: Go to Settings, find "Account", scroll to the bottom, click "Cancel plan", "Are you sure?", "We'll miss you!", confirm via email, "Wait 14 business days". Good: cancellation reachable in about as many steps as sign-up, from an obvious place. Why: asymmetric effort traps users who already decided to leave.

## Do
- Give accept and decline options comparable size, contrast and placement.
- Word the decline option neutrally ("No thanks", "Not now").
- Show the full price, including mandatory fees and taxes, on the first price display.
- Make cancellation and unsubscribe as short and discoverable as sign-up.
- Keep every control in a stable position so it can be targeted.

## Don't
- Don't use guilt-tripping copy on the decline option.
- Don't shrink or mute the decline option into a barely visible link.
- Don't add fees one by one at the last checkout step.
- Don't hide cancellation behind settings depth, retention screens, email confirmation or waiting periods.
- Don't make controls move away from the pointer.

## Voice-over summary
- The unclickable button is a dark pattern: a design trick that manipulates your choices.
- Confirmshaming makes "no" sound embarrassing so you click "yes" out of guilt.
- Hidden costs: a low price hooks you, then fees pile on at checkout.
- Roach motel: signing up takes one click, leaving is a nightmare.
- These are deliberate design choices: "Every pixel is intentional."

## Notes for the skill
- Put these into an anti-pattern list for reviews: confirmshaming copy, asymmetric visual weight of accept/decline, drip pricing, cancellation-effort asymmetry, evasive controls.
- Legal context worth noting: drip pricing and hard-to-cancel subscriptions are restricted in several jurisdictions (EU consumer law and the Digital Services Act ban deceptive interface design; US FTC rules on negative-option and junk fees). Treat these as hard "don't" rules, not style preferences.
- Accessibility: a muted, tiny decline link usually also fails text contrast (at least 4.5:1) and minimum target size (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8, 24x24 CSS px); a moving target violates predictability. Fixing the dark pattern fixes these too.
- Equal weight does not mean identical styling: a primary and a secondary button are fine, as long as the secondary is a real button with readable contrast and neutral wording.
